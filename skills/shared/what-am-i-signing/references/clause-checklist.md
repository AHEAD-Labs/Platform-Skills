# Clause checklist

Check every category. For each one, look for the one-sided signals, and if
you flag one, pair it with a realistic ask. Describe risks in practical
terms. Enforceability questions go to an attorney, not into a conclusion.

## 1. Money

Look for: payment amount, timing, trigger, method, late fees, expenses.

One-sided signals:
- Long payment terms (net 60, net 90) for a small business
- "Pay-when-paid" or "pay-if-paid": you get paid only when their client pays
- Payment "subject to approval" or "satisfaction" with no deadline to decide
- A right for them to withhold or offset money for unrelated claims
- Unilateral right to change the rate or the scope without a price change

Common asks: a shorter payment window, a deposit, milestone payments, an
objective approval deadline, late fees, and pausing work when payment is late.

## 2. Ownership of your work (intellectual property)

Look for: who owns the deliverables, when ownership transfers, what the
user keeps.

One-sided signals:
- **Assignment of everything**, including your pre-existing tools,
  templates, methods, or code (often called "background IP")
- **"Work made for hire" language.** Under U.S. copyright law, a commissioned
  work can be a work made for hire only in certain categories and only with a
  written agreement signed by both parties (17 U.S.C. § 101). If it applies,
  the client is treated as the author from the start. Flag it; the effect is
  an attorney question.
- Ownership transfers on signing or on delivery, rather than on payment
- No right for you to show the work in your portfolio
- Waiver of any right to be credited

Common asks: ownership or a license transfers only on full payment; you keep
pre-existing materials and grant a license to use them in the deliverable;
portfolio rights after public release.

## 3. Getting out (term and termination)

Look for: how long it lasts, how either side can end it, notice periods,
what is owed on termination.

One-sided signals:
- They can terminate "for convenience" at any time; you cannot
- No payment for work done before termination
- Long notice periods for you, short ones for them
- Obligations that survive termination indefinitely

Common asks: a mutual right to terminate, payment for work completed plus
non-cancellable costs, and a cancellation fee.

## 4. Auto-renewal

Look for: automatic renewal, renewal length, the notice window to cancel,
price increases on renewal.

One-sided signals:
- Renews for a full new term unless cancelled in a narrow window
- Price can rise at renewal without notice

Many states have automatic-renewal laws, but most are written to protect
consumers, not businesses. Do not assume they cover the user. Always pull
the cancel-by date into the calendar section.

Common asks: renewal only by written agreement, or a longer cancellation
window and advance notice of price changes.

## 5. Liability and indemnity

Look for: limitation of liability, indemnification, warranties, insurance.

One-sided signals:
- Their liability is capped; yours is not
- You indemnify them ("defend and hold harmless") for broad categories,
  including their own mistakes
- Warranties that results will be achieved, or that work is "fit for any
  purpose"
- Insurance requirements the user doesn't carry

Common asks: a mutual cap (often tied to fees paid), indemnity limited to
the user's own breach or infringement, and warranties limited to
professional, workmanlike performance. Wording here is an attorney item.

## 6. Restrictions on your business

Look for: non-compete, non-solicitation, exclusivity, most-favored pricing.

One-sided signals:
- A non-compete that bars working for others in the industry
- Non-solicitation broad enough to cover people who approach the user
- Exclusivity with no guaranteed volume in return
- A promise to always give them your best price

Rules on non-competes vary widely by state and by whether the person is an
employee or a contractor. Always route these to an attorney.

## 7. Confidentiality

Look for: what counts as confidential, how long it lasts, whether it is
mutual.

One-sided signals:
- One-way protection for them only
- A definition broad enough to cover the user's own know-how
- No standard exceptions (information already public, already known, or
  independently developed)

## 8. Disputes

Look for: governing law, venue, arbitration, class-action waiver, who pays
legal fees.

One-sided signals:
- A venue far from the user
- Mandatory arbitration with costs the user can't absorb
- They can recover legal fees; the user can't

## 9. Changes and assignment

One-sided signals:
- They can change the terms at any time by posting an update (common in
  platform terms)
- They can transfer the contract to anyone; you can't
- Scope can change without a written change order

## 10. Data and privacy

Look for: whether the user will handle personal data, customer lists, or
health, financial, or children's data.

If so, flag it. A data processing agreement or specific privacy obligations
may apply, and this is an attorney item.

## 11. Acceptance and approval

One-sided signals:
- Approval "at client's sole discretion" or "to client's satisfaction" with
  no deadline
- Unlimited revisions

Common asks: deemed acceptance after a set number of days without feedback,
and a set number of revision rounds.
